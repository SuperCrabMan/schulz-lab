From spikes to transcripts: How circuits recover when their inputs don’t.

We study how neurons and networks balance plasticity with stability, and what happens to uninjured circuits when the inputs they depend on are lost. Our work combines electrophysiology, single-cell molecular profiling, and computational modeling.

Research

Plasticity and stability in small neural networks

Injury or disease in one part of the nervous system can reshape networks downstream that were never directly damaged. We ask how loss of input changes the properties of neurons within a network, and how those changes help, or hinder, recovery of function.

01

Recovery after injury

After complete transection of the nerve cord, the medicinal leech regains the ability to crawl even though the severed inputs never regenerate. We track changes in ion channel expression and dopamine sensitivity in the ganglia below the injury as locomotion returns.

02

Coordinated ion channel expression

Identified neurons in crustacean motor circuits keep the mRNAs for different ion channels in tight relationships. We study the cell-type-specific feedback that creates and constrains these correlations, and how activity reshapes them.

03

From transcripts to circuit output

Single-cell RNA-seq and custom qPCR panels, paired with recordings from the same identified neurons, let us connect gene expression to excitability. Computational models test how conductance relationships produce stable network output.

ElectrophysiologyIntracellular and extracellular recordings and voltage clamp of identified neurons in intact networks.
Molecular profilingSingle-cell transcriptomics and quantitative PCR of ion channel and receptor genes.
Computational modelingBiologically constrained neuron and network models built from our own measurements.

David J. Schulz, PhD

Principal Investigator · Director & Professor of Biological Sciences

David Schulz has been on the faculty at the University of Missouri since 2005. He is the Director of the Division of Biological Sciences and the Interdisciplinary Neuroscience Program. He received his PhD from the University of Illinois at Urbana-Champaign in 2001.
